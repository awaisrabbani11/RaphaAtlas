Health: biomarkers, screening, and knowing what actually matters

Reviewed by Dr. Ahmed Humayon, MBBS Updated 30 August 2026 How we review

Most people meet their health data once a year, in a lab report full of numbers nobody explains. This page covers the measurements that genuinely predict outcomes, how often they are worth checking, how to read a result without panicking over a single decimal, and the symptoms that should never wait for an appointment.

The handful of numbers that carry most of the signal

Clinics can measure hundreds of things. In practice, a small set of markers accounts for most of what is predictable about cardiovascular and metabolic risk — and most of what you can actually change. If you only ever track a few numbers, track these.

MarkerGenerally healthy rangeWhat it is telling you
Blood pressureBelow 120/80 mmHgThe single most modifiable driver of stroke and heart disease risk. It causes no symptoms until it has done damage, which is exactly why it is measured so often.
HbA1cBelow 5.7%Average blood glucose over roughly three months. 5.7–6.4% is the prediabetes band; 6.5% and above meets the diabetes threshold on repeat testing.
Fasting glucose70–99 mg/dLA single-moment reading. 100–125 mg/dL is impaired fasting glucose; 126 mg/dL or higher, confirmed twice, indicates diabetes.
LDL cholesterolBelow 100 mg/dLThe cholesterol fraction most closely tied to arterial plaque. Targets fall lower if you already have heart disease or diabetes.
TriglyceridesBelow 150 mg/dLRises quickly with excess alcohol, refined carbohydrate, and untreated insulin resistance — one of the faster markers to respond to change.
HDL cholesterolAbove 40 mg/dL (men) / 50 mg/dL (women)Associated with lower risk, though raising it with drugs has not proven protective. Treat it as a marker, not a target.
Waist circumferenceUnder 40 in (men) / 35 in (women)Visceral fat around the organs is more metabolically active than fat elsewhere. Waist often flags risk that weight alone misses.
eGFR60 mL/min/1.73m² or aboveKidney filtration. Declines quietly for years, so a downward trend matters more than any one value.
The pattern beats the point

A single result is a snapshot taken on one morning, after one night's sleep, in one state of hydration. A trend across three readings is evidence. Before acting on any borderline number, get it repeated — clinicians do this routinely, and so should you.

How often screening is actually worth doing

More testing is not better testing. Every test carries a false-positive rate, and a false positive leads to follow-up scans, biopsies, cost, and weeks of avoidable fear. The cadences below reflect mainstream guideline consensus for adults at average risk. Personal or family history moves all of them earlier.

ScreeningWho and whenInterval
Blood pressureAll adults 18+Every 3–5 years under 40 if normal; annually from 40, or any age if elevated
Lipid panelAdults 40–75; earlier with diabetes, smoking, or family historyEvery 4–6 years if stable
Diabetes (HbA1c)Adults 35–70 with overweight or obesityEvery 3 years if normal
Colorectal cancerBegins at 45 for average riskColonoscopy every 10 years, or stool-based testing annually
Cervical cancerAges 21–65Cytology every 3 years, or HPV co-testing every 5 years from 30
Breast cancerWomen 40–74Mammogram every 2 years
Lung cancerAges 50–80 with a 20 pack-year history, current or quit within 15 yearsLow-dose CT annually

Guidelines differ between countries and between the bodies that issue them, and they change as evidence accumulates — breast screening in particular has moved more than once in recent years. Treat the table as a conversation starter with your own clinician, not a schedule to self-administer.

Reading a lab report without spiralling

Lab reports flag anything outside the reference range, and reference ranges are built to contain about 95% of a healthy population. That construction has a consequence people rarely have explained to them: run a panel of twenty independent markers on a perfectly healthy person and, on probability alone, roughly one will come back flagged. A single asterisk is weak evidence of anything.

Three habits make a report far easier to interpret:

  • Read the direction, not just the flag. A value one unit outside the range means something very different from one that is double it. Ask how far out it sits, not merely whether it is out.
  • Compare against your own history. A ferritin of 45 ng/mL is unremarkable in isolation, but if yours was 140 last year, the change is the finding — even though neither number is flagged.
  • Check the units and the lab. Reference ranges are assay-specific. Results from two different labs are not always directly comparable, which is why clinicians prefer serial testing at one lab.

Some markers also swing for reasons that have nothing to do with disease. Ferritin and CRP both rise with any recent infection. ALT rises after unaccustomed heavy exercise. TSH varies with time of day. Creatinine moves with hydration and muscle mass. A result taken the morning after a hard training session or a stomach bug deserves a repeat before it earns a diagnosis.

Symptoms that should not wait

Seek emergency care now

Chest pain or pressure, especially with exertion, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to the jaw or arm. Sudden weakness or numbness on one side, facial droop, or slurred speech. A sudden, severe headache unlike any you have had. Breathlessness at rest. Coughing or vomiting blood. Sudden loss of vision. Fainting without clear cause.

These are not conditions to research first. Call emergency services.

A second tier warrants an appointment within days rather than an ambulance: unexplained weight loss, blood in stool or urine, a lump that is new or changing, a cough lasting more than three weeks, a mole that changes shape or colour, persistent fever, or unusual fatigue that rest does not resolve. None of these confirms anything serious. All of them are worth a professional look rather than a search engine.

What prevention actually buys you

The interventions with the strongest evidence behind them are unglamorous and mostly free. Not smoking is by a wide margin the highest-value health decision available to an adult. Keeping blood pressure controlled prevents more strokes than any supplement will. Regular resistance and aerobic training improves insulin sensitivity, bone density, and mortality risk simultaneously. Sleeping seven to nine hours affects glucose control, appetite regulation, and immune function together. Vaccination prevents illness that treatment can only manage after the fact.

These overlap heavily, which is the encouraging part: improving sleep tends to improve appetite regulation, which improves body composition, which improves blood pressure and HbA1c together. Common questions

One value on my blood test was flagged. Should I worry?

Usually not on its own. Reference ranges are set to include about 95% of healthy people, so a marginal flag on a multi-marker panel is statistically expected even in someone with nothing wrong. What matters is how far outside the range it sits, whether it has moved from your previous results, and whether it fits any symptoms. Ask for a repeat before drawing conclusions.

Are full-body scans and executive health panels worth paying for?

For people without symptoms or specific risk factors, the evidence is not encouraging. Broad imaging finds a high rate of incidental abnormalities that are harmless but cannot be ignored once seen, leading to follow-up procedures with real cost and real risk. Most screening guidelines deliberately recommend targeted tests for defined groups rather than scanning everyone.

How long does it take for lifestyle changes to show up in bloodwork?

It varies by marker. Triglycerides and fasting glucose can shift within two to four weeks. Blood pressure often responds within four to eight weeks of consistent training and reduced sodium. HbA1c reflects roughly three months of average glucose, so it needs at least twelve weeks to register a real change. Retesting sooner than that mostly measures noise.

Does a normal weight mean my metabolic health is fine?

Not necessarily. Body weight says nothing about where fat is stored, and visceral fat around the organs carries more metabolic risk than fat elsewhere. People within a normal BMI can still have elevated triglycerides, raised fasting glucose, and high blood pressure. Waist circumference and a basic blood panel tell you considerably more than the scale.

Which health information sources are actually reliable?

Prefer national health services and specialty bodies — organisations that publish their methodology, date their guidance, and revise it openly. Be cautious with any source selling the solution it recommends.
